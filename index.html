What is new

Gaussian splatting normally needs an optimisation per scene or per frame: minutes to hours offline, or seconds per frame for streaming methods. This work builds a performer's Gaussians live, at 30 fps, directly from a multi-camera depth rig, with no training step, and records them as standard splats. Specifically:

  • Gaussians from fusion, not optimisation. Depth from 8–10 sensors is fused on the GPU into a confidence-weighted surface each frame. Every surface element becomes a Gaussian with its true size, lying flat along the measured surface, in ~15–22 ms per frame.
  • Colour sharper than the depth grid. Each Gaussian takes its colour from the full-resolution colour images of the cameras facing it, plus a colour slope across the splat. That keeps edges inside a 4 mm splat (eyes, lips, knit) that one flat colour per splat averages away.
  • Recorded as a standard splat sequence. Each take is written frame by frame as SPZ (1.7 MB per frame) and plays back in Unity and in the browser next to optimised 3DGS scenes.
  • Scored like 3DGS. On a camera left out of the fusion, the live result beats the offline 4DGaussians on a single performer (21.5 vs 17.0 dB) and trails it on two fast dancers (22.7 vs 23.7 dB), with the same cameras, frames and scoring for both.

Next step (work in progress): keep Gaussians alive across frames, so that a small optimisation step can run live on top of the fusion.

Abstract

Most Gaussian-splatting pipelines optimise each scene or each frame offline, which takes minutes to hours. We instead build Gaussians directly from a calibrated multi-camera RGB-D rig, frame by frame, in real time. Depth from ten sensors is fused into a persistent, confidence-weighted surface model. Each surface element becomes a Gaussian aligned to the local surface normal, with its true projected size and colour gathered from the full-resolution colour images of all cameras, including a per-splat colour slope. The live result renders in Unity at 30 fps and records to a compact sequence of standard SPZ frames that plays back next to optimised 3DGS environments.

This is real-time Gaussian-splat volumetric capture: fusion drawn as Gaussians, not optimised 3DGS. On cameras left out of the fusion it is ahead of the offline 4DGaussians on a single performer (21.5 vs 17.0 dB, averaged over three held-out cameras) and behind it on two fast dancers (22.7 vs 23.7 dB). A depth-anchored offline refinement of the recorded Gaussians adds +0.2 to +1.1 dB.

Held-out camera evaluation

One of the ten cameras is left out of the fusion entirely: no depth, no colour, no carving. The Gaussians built from the other nine are rendered from the left-out camera's pose and compared with its real photo, the standard novel-view test. For reference, 4DGaussians, an offline method, is trained on exactly the same nine cameras and frames and rendered from the same pose. The performer is outlined in the left-out camera's photos by SAM 2, which sees only the photos, not either method's output; both images are masked to that outline and cropped to it. Real-time: ~22 ms per fused frame, live. 4DGaussians: 23–34 min of training per take of ~2 s, 14,000 iterations. 4DGaussians fits its nine training views almost exactly (~31 dB) but breaks up between them, most where few training cameras see the surface (the front, without camera 0). The real-time fusion loses its points to thin holes along silhouettes and to softness and 1–2 px misregistration of fine texture; without the front camera, its face also degrades.

Second take: two dancers, eight cameras

A different rig and a harder subject: eight Azure Kinect cameras (no overhead camera), two people dancing in contact, subject ~1.4 m away. Same test: camera 0 (front) left out of the fusion and of 4DGaussians' training, both scored against its photos inside SAM 2 outlines of both dancers. The cameras agree well (92–100 % of each camera's depth points land on the others' silhouettes); the difficulty is the motion.

Here the offline method is ahead. The dancers move several centimetres per frame, so only 10–13 % of each frame's samples match an existing Gaussian (about 70 % for the guitarist): most Gaussians are rebuilt from a single frame, and the surfaces the two dancers hide from each other stay open. 4DGaussians, optimised over the whole clip, fills them but blurs and doubles the faces. Holes are 9 % of the dancers' pixels and half of the real-time error. Performer-only scores at 640×360, LPIPS VGG.

Recording and playback

  • One .gseq file per take: a small header and index, then one standard SPZ frame per capture frame. The latest take: 62 frames, 102 MB, ~140,000 Gaussians and 1.7 MB per frame.
  • Paced recording: the MKV source is stepped one frame at a time and each frame is fused and written before the next is read, so the file plays at the source's 30 fps however slow writing is. In the latest take one camera skipped a frame; the recorder waited 2 s, logged it as missing and moved on (61 consecutive frames after it).
  • Only Gaussians seen in the current or previous frame are written (~140,000 of the ~200,000 the live view keeps), so a Gaussian left behind by motion is not recorded at full opacity.
  • Optional temporal opacity: Gaussians fade in at birth and out before death instead of popping; about 22 % are born and 22 % die per frame.
  • Optional .g4d: one record per continuous stretch of each Gaussian's life instead of one per frame — 55 MB, 54 % of the .gseq, average stretch 3 frames.
  • Y-up export; plays in Unity next to optimised 3DGS environments, and in the browser viewer above.

Work in progress: holes and persistence

Two problems remain in the live fusion: surface no camera measured leaves holes, and Gaussians are rebuilt so often that a live optimisation step has nothing to build on. Both are being worked on; the numbers below are live measurements from the fusion's own logs, not yet held-out scores.

Hole filling (four switches, each optional)

  • Screen-space fill. Gaps of up to 6 px with splats around them take the surrounding colour when drawn (~2,500 px a frame). Cosmetic: nothing is added in 3D or to recordings.
  • Silhouette fill. Where a camera has no depth (hair, dark clothing) but its depth surrounds the spot on one surface, a Gaussian is placed there and coloured from the photos of the cameras facing it (~3,000 a frame). Any camera that sees through it removes it.
  • Hidden-part memory. A Gaussian no camera can currently see is kept for up to 1.5 s instead of fading, until a camera sees it again or sees the space empty.
  • Geometric body prior. Holes are closed from their rim: small ones grow one voxel a frame along the surface; big ones are spanned by curved bridges from rim to rim and relaxed into a smooth membrane. A piece is kept only inside a hole (measured surface around it on at least three of four axes) and only where no camera sees through it, sees background, or sees a surface cleanly. Its colour is blended from the rim. No learned body model is used.

What did not work, and was changed: growing every edge filled the whole body with a second, blurred layer (Gaussian count doubled); taking colour from the photos for guessed surface picked up the wall behind (white patches); flat extensions of curved surfaces left fins past the edges. Each is now prevented by the rules above.

The two fixes: every match now counts towards a Gaussian being established (still surface was treated as new and cut two frames after its first miss), and an observation that lands in a neighbouring voxel takes over that voxel's Gaussian instead of creating a new one (depth noise moves still surface across voxel boundaries). Ranges are over ten-frame windows of one take; fusion time is unchanged (~15 ms). What remains is real motion: the dancers move 3–10 cm between frames, while a Gaussian can only be matched within ~9 mm. A first motion-prediction attempt, matching along each camera's ray, failed at this speed (the fitted motion was wrong and cost 8 ms); next are constant-velocity prediction and scene flow from per-camera optical flow lifted to 3D with the cameras' depth. A live optimisation step is planned once most Gaussians live for a second or more.

Limitations

  • Live view is not optimised 3DGS. Detail is limited by depth-sensor quality and the 4 mm fusion grid, and texture seen by different cameras is misplaced by 1–2 pixels.
  • Refinement improves the recording, not the live view. Run after a recording (~4 s per frame), a depth-anchored refinement of the recorded Gaussians adds +0.2 to +1.1 dB on cameras left out of fusion and refinement. Spreading the same steps over the live loop (1–9 camera views per frame) gave no measurable gain, most likely because Gaussians are rebuilt too often to collect enough steps (only 27 % survive a frame on the dancers), and because little GPU time is left (fusion already takes ~38 ms per frame on the 8-camera rig).
  • Fast motion. With two people dancing, about three quarters of the Gaussians are rebuilt every frame (41 % for the guitarist), so the dancers look sparser and softer, and 4DGaussians, optimised over the whole clip, scores higher on that take (23.7 vs 22.7 dB). A Gaussian left behind by fast motion disappears only when its fade timer runs out.
  • Holes. Surfaces no camera sees well have no splats (behind an instrument, between the legs, between two people in contact), and surfaces seen only edge-on leave thin holes along the silhouette. A relaxed edge-birth rule cut the dancers’ holes from 4.3 to 3.5 % of their pixels (+0.5 dB); what remains (2.7 % on the guitarist) is surface no camera measured, which refinement cannot add. The hole fills under work in progress are not yet scored on a held-out camera, and guessed surface is a plausible continuation, not measured detail.
  • Short-lived Gaussians. On the dancers almost no Gaussian lives a second (median age 2–7 frames), which is why a live optimisation step cannot accumulate yet.
  • One camera faces the performer. Without it the face breaks up, and refinement helps that view least (+0.2 dB).
  • Cameras differ in colour response. A per-image colour fit recovers 1–3 dB on a held-out camera; only calibrating every camera against a colour chart fixes that, not refinement.
  • Settings are per rig. The 10-camera and 8-camera rigs need their own capture box and edge-birth and fade settings, and refinement’s best number of steps depends on how well the cameras cover each view (30 is safe on every take measured).
  • Evaluation so far. Held-out takes on two rigs (one or two performers, up to 6 s), scored inside SAM 2 outlines at 640×360 against one offline baseline (4DGaussians).
